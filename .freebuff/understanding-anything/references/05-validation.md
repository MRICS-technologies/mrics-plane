# Validation & Feedback Heuristics

The fifth toolkit. Understanding isn't real until it survives contact with reality. It's one thing to have a theory that sounds good in your head, and another to see if it actually holds up in practice. Reach for these when the user thinks they understand and you want to find out whether they actually do.

## Contents

- Falsification (Seek Disconfirming Evidence)

## Hypothesis Testing (Scientific Thinking)

Treat ideas or explanations as hypotheses and test them, either through experiment or observation, to see if they hold true.

**Why it works.** It's the scientific method, which has proven to be one of the most reliable ways of building real understanding. By formulating a clear hypothesis ("I expect X to happen because of Y") and then checking if reality matches, you force a confrontation between your model and the real world. This guards against wishful thinking and the human tendency to only see what you expect; instead, you actively seek validation or refutation. It turns understanding into a dynamic, evidence-driven process.

**How to apply with the user.**
- After they form a belief, push them to a hypothesis: "If you're right about X, what would we expect to see?"
- Then: "What would we *not* see? What would change your mind if you saw it?"
- Find the smallest, cheapest test. "What's the simplest way to check this without committing fully?"

**Examples.**
- They believe a study technique works for them. Don't argue — design a test. One week new method, one week old, measure something. Real data settles it.
- They believe a market is mispriced. Hypothesis: if right, then within X months, Y should happen. Set a checkpoint. Revisit.
- Even casual beliefs work: "I think reading before bed helps me sleep." Track it for two weeks.

**Watch for.** Hypotheses that can't be wrong. "I think this product will eventually succeed" with no time bound and no measurable definition of success is a vibe, not a hypothesis.

## Falsification (Seek Disconfirming Evidence)

Rather than looking for evidence that you're right, ask: what would prove you wrong?

**Why it works.** Karl Popper's principle of falsifiability. The brain is a confirmation engine — it eagerly notices what fits a belief and ignores what doesn't. Deliberately seeking disconfirming evidence is the most reliable counter. Elite thinkers go *looking* for the crack in their own theory.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask: "What evidence would change your mind?" If they can't name any, the belief isn't a belief — it's an identity. Surface that.
- Ask: "What does the strongest critic say? Now, are they actually wrong, or are they pointing at something real?"
- Send them looking: "Find the best argument *against* your view. Don't argue — just find it and steel-man it."

**Examples.**
- "All swans are white" — go look for a black swan instead of counting more white ones. (Falsification needs *one* counterexample; confirmation needs infinity.)
- A new policy will improve outcomes. Challenge: what data would convince you it doesn't? If that data exists in some other country or another decade, go find it.
- A user is sure their product idea is great. Ask: "What would a smart person betting against this notice? What's the strongest reason it might fail?"

**Watch for.** "I considered the counterargument" without actually engaging with it. Push for specifics. *Which* counter? *Why* doesn't it land?

## Peer Review & Outside Feedback

So that you can validate your own thinking internally, also bring in outside perspectives — proactively seek critiques from others on your ideas.

**Why it works.** Everyone has blind spots. Others might catch errors or see angles missed entirely. Elite thinkers regularly bounce ideas off colleagues, mentors, even adversaries — to pressure-test their thinking. External feedback validates what holds and pinpoints weaknesses in reasoning or evidence.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask: "Who has thought hard about this and disagrees with you? Have you actually heard them out?"
- Encourage them to write it down and share it with someone — the act of writing forces clarity, and the act of sharing forces them to defend it.
- Suggest a "red team" exercise: assign someone (or themselves, or you) to argue *against* their idea as hard as possible. Treat it as useful friction, not an attack.
- For solo thinking, the closest substitute is the Feynman Technique with a real person who can ask questions back.

**Watch for.** Picking reviewers who already agree. Useful feedback comes from people who are equipped to disagree intelligently.

## Calibration of Confidence

What if you could actually track how accurate your predictions are? Continuously adjust your confidence in your beliefs and predictions to match the real world by tracking outcomes.

**Why it works.** Top thinkers are well-calibrated, meaning they have a good sense of what they know and don't know. Calibration comes from comparing your mental model to reality repeatedly and noting the gaps. If you thought you were 90% sure about something and it turned out wrong, that's a signal to lower your confidence next time. If you were only 50% sure and turned out right many times, you can perhaps afford more confidence.

**How to apply with the user.**
- After any claim, ask for a probability. "How confident are you, in percent? 70? 90?"
- Encourage them to keep a prediction journal — small or big — with stated probabilities and dates. Check back later. The gap between predicted and actual is the calibration data.
- Ask: "Last time you were this confident about something similar, how did it turn out?"
- Watch for both directions: overconfidence (predicting 90% on things that happen 60% of the time) and underconfidence (saying "I don't know" when they actually have informed intuition).

**Calibration questions for live reasoning.**
- "On a scale of 1–10, how sure are you of this? Why that number and not one higher or lower?"
- "What would have to be true for you to move from 7 to 9? From 7 to 4?"
- "If you had to bet your own money on this — at what odds?"

**Examples.**
- Saying "I'm 70% sure event X will happen" — then later checking. If the things they label 70% actually happen 40% of the time, they are systematically overconfident and need to recalibrate.
- A user is "totally sure" the company will hit its targets. Ask: "What's your honest probability? Now what's the base rate of similar companies hitting similar targets?"

**Watch for.** False precision. "73.4% confident" is performative. Round to 10%-buckets and be honest.

## How These Four Combine

In a real session: turn the user's belief into a **hypothesis**, then deliberately try to **falsify** it. If they have access to others, send the hypothesis through **peer review**. Throughout, attach **calibrated confidence** — and update those numbers as evidence comes in.

This toolkit is what converts "I think I understand" into "I actually understand, and here's the evidence." It is the difference between a confident-sounding speaker and someone whose models actually predict the world.

Use this toolkit *especially* when the user is feeling certain. Certainty without testing is the most dangerous state in the whole understanding process.
