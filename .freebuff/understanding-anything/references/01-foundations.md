# Foundations of Understanding

The first toolkit. These are the frameworks for breaking a topic down to its source and tracing how the pieces fit. Reach for them at the start of almost any deep-understanding session — they put a foundation under everything that comes after.

## First Principles Thinking

Reduce the problem or topic to its most basic, indisputable elements and build up from there — instead of accepting existing assumptions or analogies.

**Why it works.** When we begin from someone else's framing, we inherit their mistakes. Working from foundational truths means the mental model is grounded in things that are actually true, not things people have repeated.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask: "What do we know is true about this without taking anyone's word for it?"
- Ask: "If we threw away every textbook explanation of this, what would we still be sure of?"
- When they cite a fact, ask why it is true. Keep going until they hit a primitive — a physical law, a definition, an observation that doesn't decompose further.
- Once they have a small set of primitives, ask: "Now build the rest of the topic up from these. What follows?"

**Example moves.**
- Topic: rockets. Primitive: action and reaction (Newton's third law). Build up: thrust requires expelling mass; expelling mass requires fuel; carrying fuel adds mass; therefore the tyranny of the rocket equation. Suddenly the design constraints feel obvious instead of arbitrary.
- Topic: inflation. Primitive: prices are ratios between money and goods. Build up: more money chasing the same goods → ratio shifts → prices rise. From there every monetary policy debate has a frame.

**Watch for.** Users smuggling in conclusions as primitives. "Markets are efficient" is not a primitive; it is a claim. Push back gently.

## Decomposition (Divide & Conquer)

Break a big complex system into smaller bite-sized parts and study them one at a time, then study how they interact.

**Why it works.** Working memory is finite. A topic with 32 moving parts is incomprehensible until you chunk it into 4 groups of 8. The brain can hold 4 things; it cannot hold 32.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask: "What are the main parts of this? Don't worry about being right — just list what you think the pieces are."
- For each part, ask: "What does this part do, in one sentence?"
- Then: "Which parts interact with which? Draw the lines."
- Treat each part as its own mini-topic. Study one. Then the next.
- Reassemble: "Now that you've looked at each piece, what does the whole do that no single piece does?"

**Example moves.**
- Topic: an ecosystem. Decompose into producers, consumers, decomposers, climate, geography, food webs. Study each, then dive into one interaction (e.g., a predator–prey pair) in detail, then zoom back out.
- Topic: a startup that isn't growing. Decompose into acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, referral. Find which link is breaking. Study that link.

**Watch for.** Decomposing too far. The point is comprehension, not infinite reduction. Stop at the level where each piece is still meaningful on its own.

## Identifying Key Drivers (Pareto Principle)

Focus on the small subset of factors or principles that have an outsized impact on the system or problem (the 20% that drives 80% of outcomes).

**Why it works.** Complex systems often have many variables, but not all are equally important. Elite thinkers prioritise the high-leverage points (the core drivers) which yields a more powerful understanding of how the system really behaves.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Once they've decomposed, ask: "If you could only understand three of these parts, which three would tell you the most about the whole?"
- Ask: "If one variable changed by a lot, which one would change the system the most?"
- Force a ranking. They will resist — push them. Ranking is where the real thinking happens.

**Example moves.**
- Topic: why a company is profitable. Possible drivers: pricing, volume, cost structure, market position, regulation, brand. Force the user to pick the top two. Argue the picks.
- Topic: climate. Drivers include CO₂, methane, albedo, ocean currents, solar variability. Concentrate on what really moves the needle, not what gets the most press.

**Watch for.** "Everything matters" is the enemy of understanding. Insist on a ranking even if it's rough.

## Structural Mapping

Sketch out — literally or mentally — how the parts of a system are connected. The structure is the understanding.

**Why it works.** Once you see the structure, relationships jump out at you. Patterns become visible. You stop memorising facts and start seeing the shape that generates the facts.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask them to draw it. Even a rough mental picture works. "Sketch how these connect — boxes and arrows are fine."
- Ask: "What flows along these connections? Energy, money, information, force, decisions?"
- Ask: "Where are the loops? Where does an output feed back as an input?"
- Ask: "Where are the bottlenecks? Where is the fragile link?"

**Example moves.**
- Topic: photosynthesis. Map: sunlight → chlorophyll → splits water → produces oxygen and ATP → ATP drives carbon fixation → glucose. Once mapped, every confusing detail finds its place.
- Topic: a market. Map: supply, demand, prices, expectations, policy. Show the loops — high prices reduce demand, but expectations of higher prices increase demand short-term. Suddenly bubbles make sense.

**Watch for.** Lists masquerading as maps. A list is not a structure. If there are no arrows, there is no structure yet.

## Levels of Abstraction (Zoom In & Out)

Understand a topic at multiple levels — from the big-picture context down to the fine-grained details — and learn to zoom between them at will.

**Why it works.** A single moment fixed at one level traps you. Zoom too far in and you see mechanism but lose meaning. Zoom too far out and you see narrative but lose mechanism. Elite thinkers move fluidly between levels.

**How to apply with the user.**
- For any topic they describe, ask: "What's this part of, one level up?" and "What's inside this, one level down?"
- Ask: "If you were explaining this to someone who only had 30 seconds, what's the one-sentence version? Now if they had an hour, what would you add?"
- When stuck on a detail, deliberately zoom out: "Forget the mechanism for a second — why does this part exist at all?"
- When stuck on the big picture, deliberately zoom in: "Pick one concrete instance of this. Walk through what happens."

**Example moves.**
- Topic: a cell. Zoom in: organelles → proteins → atoms. Zoom out: tissue → organ → organism → species → ecosystem. Each level explains things the others can't.
- Topic: a war. Zoom in: a single decision by a single commander. Zoom out: the geopolitical pressures that made the war structurally inevitable.

**Watch for.** Users getting stuck at one level. Some people live in the abstract; others can't leave the concrete. Force the move they resist.

---

## How These Five Combine

In a real session you will weave them together: decompose into parts → identify the key driver among the parts → apply first principles to that driver → map how it connects to the rest → zoom out to see why the whole behaves as it does. None of these alone is enough. Together, they put a foundation under any topic.
