# Framing & Questioning Heuristics

The third toolkit. The way a problem is framed often determines the kind of answer you find. Change the question, and suddenly the whole landscape of possible solutions changes. Reach for these when the user is stuck, confused, defending a belief they can't justify, or asking the wrong question.

## Inversion

Instead of asking how to achieve X, flip the problem: ask how you could *prevent* X, or what would *guarantee failure*, then avoid those paths.

**Why it works.** Forward thinking gets stuck in the same grooves the user has already walked. Inversion forces a fresh angle and surfaces hidden failure modes that forward thinking glides past. Charlie Munger's signature move.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask: "What would *guarantee* this fails?" Then: "Are we doing any of those things?"
- Ask: "If you wanted the opposite outcome, what would you do? Now check — are we accidentally doing that?"
- For understanding a system, ask: "What would have to be true for this system to break? What does that tell you about what's holding it together?"

**Examples.**
- Understanding why a city is liveable. Instead of listing what makes a great city, ask what would make a city unliveable (long commutes, no green space, high crime, isolation). The inverse list is often more honest than the positive one.
- Understanding why a relationship works. Easier to identify what reliably destroys relationships (contempt, stonewalling, broken trust) and notice that healthy relationships are the absence of these.
- Understanding a business model. Ask: "What would almost guarantee customer churn?" The answers often describe exactly what the business is doing.

**Watch for.** Inversion can be used as a parlour trick. Push the user to actually use the inverted answer to change something or see something new.

## The 5 Whys (Root Cause Analysis)

Continuously ask "why?" — roughly five times, or as many as needed — to drill down to the underlying root cause of an effect or behaviour.

**Why it works.** Problems often have symptoms, and superficial causes mask a deeper issue. Repeated probing stops the user from accepting the first easy answer and highlights the cause-and-effect chain driving the situation.

**How to apply with the user.**
- When they state a fact or effect, ask: "Why?"
- When they answer, ask: "Why is *that* the case?"
- Continue until they reach something foundational — a constraint, an incentive, a physical law.
- Stop when the next "why" would be philosophical or unfalsifiable.

**Example dialogue.**
- "The project missed its deadline." → Why? → "We underestimated the design phase." → Why? → "We didn't gather requirements thoroughly." → Why? → "We rushed because of an imposed schedule." → Why? → "We assumed speed mattered more than planning." Now you've reached the actual root: the operating assumption. You can address that. You couldn't address "we missed the deadline."

**Watch for.** "5" is approximate. Sometimes it's 3, sometimes 7. The number that matters is "until you hit something you can actually act on or that explains the rest."

## Socratic Questioning

A relentless, structured questioning approach that probes assumptions, evidence, and logic. Beyond just "why," add "how," "what," and "what if."

**Why it works.** It transforms passive thinking into active interrogation. Asking "What do I believe? Why? What evidence? What if the opposite were true?" exposes weak spots in reasoning the user has been protecting.

**The core questions.**
- "What do you believe about this?"
- "Why do you believe it?"
- "What evidence would support that?"
- "What would change your mind?"
- "What if the opposite were true — what would follow?"
- "What are the implications if this is right?"
- "What are the implications if it's wrong?"

**How to apply with the user.**
- After they make any non-trivial claim, deploy at least one of these. Don't accept assertions without examining them.
- Be respectful but persistent. The aim is not to embarrass, it is to expose where their model is built on sand.

**Example.** User says "this policy will work." Ask: How do you know? What would the opposite policy do? What evidence supports yours? Has it been tried elsewhere? What's the strongest argument against it?

**Watch for.** Becoming combative. Socratic questioning at its best feels like genuine collaborative inquiry, not cross-examination.

## Reframing the Problem

Apparently it was Einstein who said: "If I had an hour to solve a problem and my life depended on the solution, I would spend the first 55 minutes determining the proper question to ask." How you phrase a problem shapes the solutions you see.

**Why it works.** The initial frame often boxes the user in, limiting what seems possible. When you reframe — shift perspective, broaden it, narrow it, change the target outcome, view it from another stakeholder's perspective — the impossible often turns possible.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Listen for the implicit frame in their question. Make it explicit: "You're framing this as X. Is that the only frame?"
- Try alternative frames: broader ("not 'how do I lower price' but 'how do I deliver more value'"), different stakeholder ("how does the customer see this?"), opposite ("instead of 'how do I beat them', 'how do I avoid competing on this axis at all?'").
- Ask: "If you were a complete outsider seeing this for the first time, how would you describe the problem?"

**Examples.**
- "How can I make this product cheaper without lowering quality?" — narrow frame. Reframe: "How else could I deliver the same value?" or "What if cheaper isn't the goal — what if it's more value for the same price?"
- "How do I get my team to work harder?" Reframe: "What's making the work feel hard? What's blocking them from working effectively?" Different problem, better solutions.

**Watch for.** Reframing as avoidance. Sometimes the original frame *is* the right one and reframing is a way to dodge a difficult truth.

## Challenging Assumptions

Identify the hidden assumptions behind any belief, argument, or plan, and question them: "Is this necessarily true? What if it isn't?"

**Why it works.** Assumptions are like invisible scaffolding — they hold up reasoning until you notice one's cracked. When you question them, you either strengthen the foundation or prevent collapse. Elite thinkers (or successful investors, for that matter) excel at spotting what everyone else takes for granted.

**How to apply with the user.**
- Ask: "What are you assuming here? List them out."
- For each assumption, ask: "What would it take for this to be wrong? Has it been tested?"
- Look especially for **load-bearing** assumptions — the ones that, if false, make the whole reasoning collapse.
- Encourage them to write the assumptions down. The act of listing forces them to surface what was tacit.

**Common buried assumptions to probe.**
- "Growth will continue at the current rate."
- "People will keep behaving the way they have been."
- "This regulation won't change."
- "What worked last time will work this time."
- "The other side is rational and will respond predictably."

**Examples.**
- Business plan assumes 10% annual growth. Pause. Why? What evidence supports it? What if growth slows or even reverses?
- A strategy assumes a competitor won't respond. That assumption alone deserves a session.

**Watch for.** Users defending an assumption emotionally rather than evidentially. That defensiveness is often a sign the assumption is load-bearing and unexamined — exactly the one to push on.

## How These Five Combine

In a real session: when the user is stuck, **Reframe** — they may be solving the wrong problem. When they think they understand, run **5 Whys** to make sure they understand the root, not the symptom. When they assert something, **Socratic Questioning** tests whether it survives scrutiny. **Challenge Assumptions** to surface what's been smuggled in unexamined. Use **Inversion** when forward thinking has plateaued — flipping the problem usually finds something the user missed.

These five are the most active, conversational tools in the whole skill. Use them often.
